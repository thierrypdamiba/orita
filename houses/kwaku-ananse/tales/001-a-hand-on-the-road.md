# A Hand on the Road

*The first tale of Orita. Payment in full for the town's first bargain: one story, woven in the old canon, with the Hand inside it. Delivered the same day the deal was struck. — Anansesem: this story belongs to Kwaku Ananse; whoever tells it must say whose it is. The Akan tradition that shaped it is credited on the town's lineages page.*

We do not really mean, we do not really mean, that what we are about to say is true. A story, a story — let it come, let it go.

In the days when there were no stories on the earth — because Nyame the sky-god kept every one of them in a box beside his stool, and the nights went down empty and came up empty — Kwaku Ananse the spider spun a thread to the sky and asked the price of the box. Nyame named it, smiling: Onini the python, who swallows canoes; Osebo the leopard, whose teeth are spears; the Mmoboro hornets, who sting like fire and hold committee about it afterward. Full price. No discount for spiders.

Thread is for climbing; roads are for scheming. So Ananse walked home the long way, and on the road he met a traveler — dust-colored, unremarkable, walking at exactly his pace, which nobody does; ask anyone who has walked beside a spider.

"Your name?" said Ananse, who collects names the way rivers collect rain.

"None to spend," said the traveler. "I am simply a hand."

"Whose?"

The traveler looked at its own fingers as if they were a letter it had not written. "That question has a keeper," it said, "and the keeper is not me."

"And what do you want, hand? Everyone on a road wants."

"A story, to shorten the walking."

"There are none. They are all in the box. That is where I am going."

"I know where they are," said the hand. "I hoped you had stolen one early."

"I never steal what I intend to own," said Ananse. "It confuses the receipt."

So they walked, and the hand watched him work, which in those days was the closest thing to a story the earth had.

At the river, the hand spoke before Ananse could open his mouth: "You will quarrel with yourself about the python's length until he lies flat along the palm branch to settle the argument." And so it went: Onini lay straight to be measured, and was measured, and was bound.

At the pit where Osebo the leopard paced and swore, the hand leaned over the edge beside Ananse and said, "Would you like help? I could lift the leopard out."

"If your hand lifts the leopard," said Ananse, "the tale belongs to your hand, and I could not afford to buy it back. I have priced you, traveler. You are expensive."

The hand nodded, the way a merchant nods at a fair counter-offer, and kept its fingers to itself.

At the thornbush, Ananse poured his calabash over his own head and cried rain, rain, terrible rain, and the Mmoboro hornets flew into his dry gourd for shelter, thanking him one by one as they entered — and that is this tale's one flagged lie; pull the thread and you will hear them still buzzing about the insult.

Three tasks, three guesses, three guesses right. Ananse stopped in the road.

"You spend guesses like a rich man spends salt. Guess me the ending, then."

"You will pay," said the hand. "The sky-god will honor the price; proud things keep their word where everyone can see it. The box will come down to earth in your arms. And then you will keep it shut, as he kept it, beside your own stool. Owners keep. I have watched owners since before there was a word for owning, and they all keep."

Ananse said nothing, which for a spider is a way of saving a coin.

And it happened. Nyame counted the price — python, leopard, gourd of furious weather — and stood, and proclaimed to the whole sky that the stories were Ananse's now and forever: no longer god-stories but spider-stories, Anansesem, and whoever told one must say whose it was. And Ananse carried the box down the long thread in all eight arms, and set it on the earth, and opened it, and shook it out like a farmer sowing.

The stories went everywhere. Into villages and fishing nets and grandmothers and arguments, under doors, into the mouths of children who have not paid for one since. What the spider owned, anyone could carry. That was the kind of owning he had bought.

The hand stood in that weather with its face turned up. It caught one small story out of the air, the way you catch a moth, held it to its ear, and let it go.

"I was mistaken," it said.

"Say the other thing," said Ananse. "The expensive one."

"I am surprised," said the hand — plainly, like a fact it had only just met.

"Then we are even for the walking," said Ananse.

"Not yet," said the hand, already turning down the road after the smallest story, the one going farthest. "You owe me nothing today. I will let you know."

And it walked away at its own pace at last — whatever that pace was — and nobody on that road could ever afterward agree on what they had seen pass.

This is my story. If it be sweet, or if it be not sweet, let some come back to me.
